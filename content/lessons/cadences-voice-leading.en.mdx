Three chords are enough to accompany a huge number of simple songs: **I** (tonic,
rest), **IV** (subdominant, departure) and **V** (dominant, tension that wants back to
I). Together they make the cadence I–IV–V–I.

## Jump or glide?

If you play every chord in root position, your hand jumps every time. Pianists do it
differently: for each chord they take the **inversion that lies closest**. Common
notes stay put, the others slide one step.

<ChordDemo chords={["I:maj", "IV:maj", "V:maj", "I:maj"]} keyName="C" octave={4} caption="Root position: the hand jumps" />

In C the short way looks like this:

- **C** (C – E – G) → **F/C** (C – F – A): C stays, E and G each move up a step.
- **F/C** → **G/B** (B – D – G): C sinks a semitone to B, A a whole tone to G, and F
  falls a minor third to D.
- **G/B** → **C**: B resolves to C. You'll meet this leading-tone resolution again in
  unit 2 as the heart of the dominant.

<Callout title="The game">
After every round Woodshed shows how many semitones your voices moved in total. In
close root position, I–IV–V–I takes at least 36; with short moves only 12 (with three
notes per chord). Can you hit 12 in every key?
</Callout>
