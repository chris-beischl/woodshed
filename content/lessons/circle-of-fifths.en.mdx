If "what's the fifth of E♭?" means counting up the scale first, that isn't a talent
problem – it's a missing reflex. Pros don't calculate here, they **know**, the way you
know Wednesday comes after Tuesday. The circle of fifths is exactly that weekday
sequence for keys. The goal of this lesson: every fifth, every fourth and every key
signature in **under a second**.

## What it is

Arrange the twelve keys like a clock face. Clockwise, each step goes up a **fifth**
(C → G → D …) and adds one ♯ (up to F♯). Counter-clockwise, each step goes up a **fourth**
(C → F → B♭ …) and adds one ♭ (up to G♭). At the bottom both sides meet: F♯ major (6♯) and
G♭ major (6♭) sound the same.

The clock helps you remember: **the hour is the number of sharps.** G sits at 1 o'clock
(1♯), E at 4 o'clock (4♯). Counter-clockwise you count flats: F at 11 o'clock has 1♭,
A♭ at 8 o'clock has 4♭.

## Why it matters so much

1. **Key signatures.** The position on the circle *is* the key signature. If you know
   the circle, you never have to piece a scale together again.
2. **Neighbours are related.** Two neighbouring keys share six of their seven notes
   (C and G major differ only by F/F♯). That's why moving to a neighbour sounds smooth
   and jumping across the circle sounds dramatic.
3. **The dominant.** V → I is one step counter-clockwise: G7 → C. The strongest move in
   harmony is a move around the circle.
4. **Jazz runs in fourths.** ii–V–I is the same counter-clockwise step twice
   (Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7). Turnarounds and many standards chain these steps endlessly. Know
   the circle of fourths in your sleep and you read those changes in real time.
5. **Practising in all keys.** That's why Woodshed introduces new keys in fourths: C, F,
   B♭, E♭ … – exactly how jazz musicians practise every new pattern.

## How to learn it by heart

### 1. The fifths formula

For the fifth above a note, count five letters (C D E F **G**) and **keep the
accidental**: E♭ → B♭, A♭ → E♭, F♯ → C♯, D → A.

There is exactly **one exception**: roots on **B**. B–F is not a perfect fifth (only 6
semitones), so the fifth gets one more ♯: B → **F♯**, B♭ → **F**.

<Callout title="The whole rule in one sentence">
Fifth = five letters on, same accidental. Only on B is the fifth raised a semitone.
</Callout>

### 2. The fourths formula

For the fourth, count four letters (C D E **F**), again keeping the accidental:
E♭ → A♭, D♭ → G♭, G♭ → **C♭**. The only exception here is **F**: F–B is an augmented
fourth (6 semitones), so F → **B♭** and F♯ → B.

A fourth up is a fifth down. So you need both directions – and with these two formulas
you can travel the circle either way.

### 3. The order of the accidentals

Sharps are always added in the same order, which is itself a piece of the circle:
**F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯ E♯ B♯**. Flats are the same row backwards:
**B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ G♭ C♭ F♭**.

Two shortcuts that identify any key:

- **Sharp keys:** the last sharp is a semitone below the tonic. Last sharp D♯ → E major.
- **Flat keys:** the second-to-last flat is the tonic. B♭ E♭ A♭ → E♭ major. (F major,
  with its single ♭, you simply remember.)

<Callout title="Mnemonics">
Order of sharps: **F**ather **C**harles **G**oes **D**own **A**nd **E**nds **B**attle.
Order of flats – the same sentence backwards: **B**attle **E**nds **A**nd **D**own **G**oes
**C**harles' **F**ather.
</Callout>

### 4. A few minutes daily, not one hour once

A reflex comes from short, frequent repetition. Do the sprints below every day until you
stop thinking; afterwards the practice plan reminds you in time. Say the answers out
loud, and walk the circle away from the piano too, for example while waiting:
"C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯ … – C, F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭ …"

### 5. With your hands

Knowing it in your head isn't enough; your hands need to know the way too. So you finish
by going once around the circle with chords, counter-clockwise as in jazz. Take the
closest inversion each time; Woodshed measures your voice leading and remembers your best.
