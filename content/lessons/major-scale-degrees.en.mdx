## Musicians think in numbers

"The third", "the seventh", "ii–V–I": in jazz every note of a scale gets a **number**,
its **degree**. In C major, C is 1, D is 2, E is 3 … up to B, which is 7.

Play the scale and **say the numbers out loud**: one, two, three …

<Exercise id="scale-major" keyName="C" />

---

## Why numbers instead of names?

A name only holds in one key; a number holds in **all twelve**. "Play the 3" means E in
C major, A in F major, G in E♭ major.

Think in degrees and you can take every chord and every lick into any key. That is
exactly what this lesson trains.

---

## How it's built

The seven degrees are not evenly spaced. The pattern is always:

**Whole – Whole – Half – Whole – Whole – Whole – Half**

The two **half steps** sit between **3 and 4** and between **7 and 8** (8 is the 1
again). In C major that is E–F and B–C, the two places with no black key in between.

Play just those two places: 3, 4 – then 7, 1.

<Exercise id="degrees-half-steps" keyName="C" />

## Same pattern, every key

The pattern travels: from any root, Whole – Whole – Half – Whole – Whole – Whole – Half
gives a major scale. That's why the numbers work everywhere.

So in D major the half steps are F♯–G and C♯–D. Find them, then try more keys with
"Next".

## A word on spelling

Woodshed spells like a lead sheet, with real accidentals: **B♭**, **F♯**, and when the
key or chord calls for it even **C♭** or **B𝄫**. The symbols ♭ and ♯ are always written out.

---

## 1, 3, 5: the major triad

Take degrees **1, 3 and 5** of the scale and play them together: that's the major
triad. Chords are nothing more than degrees sounding at once.

## Every letter exactly once

The most important spelling rule: **in a major scale every letter appears exactly
once.** That's why the 4th degree of G♭ major is **C♭** and not B, even though you
press the same key:

G♭ – A♭ – B♭ – **C♭** – D♭ – E♭ – F

Play G♭ major and look at the notation afterwards: there's the C♭.

## Degree sprint: which note?

Now without the keyboard, just in your head. Eight quick questions: which note is the
3rd, the 6th … in D major? The wrong answers are mean: often the same note with the
wrong name sits right next to the answer (F♯ against G♭).

Tip: the number keys 1–4 are faster than the mouse.

## What you can do now

- Every note of a scale has a **number** that holds in every key.
- The pattern **Whole – Whole – Half – Whole – Whole – Whole – Half**, half steps at 3–4
  and 7–8.
- **1, 3, 5** make the major triad.
- **Every letter once**: hence C♭ in G♭ major.
