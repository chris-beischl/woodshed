Almost everything in jazz is thought of in **degrees**: "the third", "the seventh",
"ii–V–I". If you instantly know which note is the 3rd or the 6th degree of a key, you
can take every chord and every lick into all 12 keys. That is what this lesson is about.

## How it's built

A major scale has seven notes with the step pattern

**Whole – Whole – Half – Whole – Whole – Whole – Half**

So the half steps sit between the 3rd and 4th and between the 7th and 8th degree. In
C major that is exactly E–F and B–C, the two places without a black key.

<Callout title="Spelling">

key calls for it even **C♭** or **B𝄫**.
</Callout>

## Every letter exactly once

The most important rule for reading music correctly: **in a major scale every letter
name appears exactly once.** That is why the 4th degree of G♭ major is called **C♭**
and not B, even though you press the same key:

G♭ – A♭ – B♭ – **C♭** – D♭ – E♭ – F

Sounds pedantic, but it is the key to everything that follows: only then is the third
of a chord right, and only then can you read a lead sheet fluently.

<Callout title="Why the cycle of fourths?">
Woodshed introduces new keys in the order C, F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, B, E, A, D, G.
That is how jazz musicians practise ii–V–Is: each new key is a fourth higher, just
like the chord movement in jazz.
</Callout>
