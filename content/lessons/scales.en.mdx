Every bebop line, every smooth-jazz run is made of scale fragments. If you can play a
scale blind and evenly, your fingers are free later for what really counts: rhythm
and expression. That's why the scales come right after the degrees, in all 12 keys.

## Right-hand fingering

The thumb passes under after the 3 or the 4 and should land on a white key where
possible. That leaves only a few patterns:

| Keys | Right-hand fingering (ascending) |
|---|---|
| C, G, D, A, E, B | 1 2 3 – 1 2 3 4 5 |
| F | 1 2 3 4 – 1 2 3 4 |
| B♭ | 4 – 1 2 3 – 1 2 3 4 |
| E♭ | 3 – 1 2 3 4 – 1 2 3 |
| A♭ | 3 4 – 1 2 3 – 1 2 3 |
| D♭ | 2 3 – 1 2 3 4 – 1 2 |
| G♭ | 2 3 4 – 1 2 3 – 1 2 |

<Callout title="One rule for all flat keys">
In F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭ and G♭ the right hand's 4th finger always lands on B♭. If you
remember one sentence, make it this one.
</Callout>

Descending, play the same fingering backwards. The 5th finger only goes on the top
note. Woodshed can't see your fingers, so watch them yourself: better slow with the
right fingering than fast with the wrong one.

## Minor: the relative

The **natural minor scale** has the same notes as its relative major; it just starts
on that scale's 6th degree. So A minor has the notes of C major, and E♭ minor the
notes of G♭ major:

E♭ – F – G♭ – A♭ – B♭ – **C♭** – D♭ – E♭

In minor-scale degrees: 1 – 2 – ♭3 – 4 – 5 – ♭6 – ♭7. Unit 4 adds harmonic and melodic
minor, together with the reason they exist at all.

## The game

One octave up and back down, in any register. The dots fill up note by note. After a
mistake Woodshed shows you the expected note and you simply carry on from there.
Clean runs earn a **best time** per key. Beat it.
