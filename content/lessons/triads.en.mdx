A triad is two thirds stacked on top of each other. Whether each third is major
(4 semitones) or minor (3 semitones) decides its character.

| Triad | lower third | upper third | Degrees | Symbol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Major | major | minor | 1 – 3 – 5 | C |
| Minor | minor | major | 1 – ♭3 – 5 | Cm |
| Diminished | minor | minor | 1 – ♭3 – ♭5 | C° |
| Augmented | major | major | 1 – 3 – ♯5 | C+ |

Listen to the four one after another. Notice how small changes to the third and fifth
tip the character:

<Callout title="What about half-diminished?">
It exists, but it isn't a triad – it's a **four-note chord**: the diminished triad
plus a minor seventh, so C – E♭ – G♭ – B♭ (Cm7♭5, often written Cø7). With a
diminished seventh (B𝄫) it becomes the fully diminished C°7. You'll meet both in
unit 2, where the half-diminished chord is needed as ii in minor.
</Callout>

## Why this matters for jazz

Triads look plain, but they are the raw material for the most beautiful jazz sounds:
a D major triad over C – E – B in the left hand gives the floating, Lydian sound
Cmaj13(♯11) that you'll meet in unit 5 as an "upper structure". If you can grab triads
blind in every key, those sounds will be under your fingers straight away.

## Spelling

Letter arithmetic applies here too: E♭ minor is E♭ – **G♭** – B♭ (not F♯), and C° is
C – E♭ – **G♭**. The answer after each attempt shows you the correct notation.

## Practice

Inversion and spacing don't matter: root position or inversion, close or open, notes
may be doubled. All three notes must sound, and no foreign note may be in there.
